<p style="text-align: left;">Writing, as it has come to be known, will continue to be an invaluable skill, as has always been reading; but in a fast-paced world, both individuals and establishments will try to question the raison d’être of this ancient, once-acclaimed humane practice by making every act of writing looks like a deplorable performance aimed at meaning-making. Whether one likes it or not, certain performances cannot be negotiated: ideas will flourish and minds will be nurtured, as long as the clock ticks in our modern world. </p><p style="text-align: left;">Interestingly, this is a world where writers do not control how their readers feel about the texts they interact with. This is a world where narratives are censored, especially if these are deemed to have been spoken for the masses or against social issues affecting people’s wellbeing. This is a world where elected officials, more often than not, attempt to influence public opinion, control the press and silent voices, loud enough, that they consider a threat. </p><p style="text-align: left;">What an interesting world! </p><p style="text-align: left;">From crafty people being accused of losing their voices to chat bots, to prolific authors being cancelled by their publishers, to academics facing harsher criticism, to emerging researchers reflecting on the relevance of authorial content, this is indeed a defining moment in history. Perhaps, in the future, written stories will no longer be contested. Authors may not need to negotiate their own places, as though before the invention of the printing machine, there had never been a way to convey meaningful ideas that mattered. One day, committees on literary prizes might find new ways to select their prize-winning writers amidst myriad of controversies in a hyper connected era. </p><p style="text-align: left;">Until then, humanity will continue to witness inventions and the rise of techno culture, all of which are rooted in consumerism. The reading culture will be redefined for sure, and the humanities will begin to see a shift in the ways material culture and consumption are examined. One thing is certain: the lived experiences, the cultural nuances, and scores of other humane traits such as empathy, consciousness, emotions, and feelings—no algorithms dare replace. </p><p style="text-align: left;">C'était ça ou mourir ?</p><img src="/blog-images/2026-11-23-post-img-9784-dvj7i.jpg" alt="IMG_9784"><p></p>
